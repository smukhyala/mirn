The working

How every number on the console is worked out

Everything described here is simulated. The crowd is an invented model of pedestrians — invented people obeying invented rules — and no number here is a measurement of real pedestrians. What is real is the ruler: the same room is run twice, once with a robot and once without, from the same starting positions and the same random wobble, and the difference between a person's two paths is the robot's effect on them.

Back to the console

How to read this

Every measurement below is set out the same way, and it starts in the same place: with what the measurement reads when the true effect is exactly nothing. That is the most useful thing anyone can tell you about a number. A reading with no stated zero cannot be judged — you cannot tell a large effect from a large habit of the ruler — and the whole argument of this site is that the second is easy to mistake for the first.

After the zero comes what the measurement is, then the arithmetic in words and in ordinary notation, then what it takes for granted and what goes wrong when that fails.

The words the formulas use

Five terms, defined once here and used everywhere below.

An instant
The simulator moves the world forward in equal ticks and writes down where everybody is after each one. An instant is one of those written-down moments. The gap in time between two of them is written Δt, and the number of instants in a run is written T. There are N people in the room.
The two runs
Every result on the console comes from a pair of runs of the same room. The two share a seed, the same people in the same starting places, and the same random wobble tick by tick. They differ in one thing only — on the console as it stands, whether the robot is there at all. So a person exists twice, and walks two paths.
The gap
For one person at one instant, the gap is the distance in metres between where they are in the run with the robot and where they are in the run without it, at that same instant. It is written d(t), or di(t) when it matters whose gap it is. Because the two runs differ in nothing else, this gap is the robot's effect on that person, at that moment. Nothing is estimated.
The curve
Average the gap over everybody in the room, instant by instant, and you get a single curve running the length of the episode: how far the crowd, on average, has been moved at each moment. It is written g(t). Two of the headline readouts are just the mean and the maximum of this one curve, which is why they can disagree so loudly.
g(t)  =  (1 / N)  ×  Σi  di(t)
The reference run in which nobody notices the robot
A second pair of runs at the very same settings, with one thing changed: people are not allowed to react to the robot. The robot still crosses the room; nobody moves out of its way. In that pair the true effect is exactly nothing, by construction, so any measurement that reports something on it is reporting something other than the robot. The console buys this pair whenever it can, and one of the readouts below exists for no other purpose than to show what it says.

The effect itself

Two numbers, both taken from the one curve defined above. One is its mean, the other its maximum. That single difference is the whole reason they can tell different stories about the same run.

How far the robot pushed the crowd

When the true effect is exactly zero It reads exactly zero, to the last digit — not nearly zero. Switch off the setting that lets people notice the robot and every gap in the room is exactly nought, because the two runs then agree tick for tick and bit for bit. That is not a coincidence of arithmetic. It is what the paired construction buys: the two runs share the same random draws, so with nothing to react to there is nothing left to differ.

What it is

The average distance, over everybody and over the whole episode, between where a person went with the robot in the room and where the same person went without it.

How it is worked out

Take one person. At each instant, measure the gap between their two positions. Average those gaps over the whole episode: that is that person's own number. Do it for everybody, and average the results. Averaging each person first and then averaging the people comes to the same thing as averaging the curve from end to end.

(1 / N)  ×  Σi  [  (1 / T)  ×  Σt  di(t)  ]

Every instant counts the same. A person standing still at the far end of the room for most of the episode contributes a long stretch of near-zero gaps, which pulls the average down as surely as a stretch of calm would.

What it assumes

That the two runs really do differ in one thing only. That is enforced rather than hoped for: the pair is built by one function from one shared tape of random numbers, and it refuses to exist if the two arms disagree about where anybody started or how long the episode ran.

What it cannot do is survive leaving this simulator. Nobody can run a real corridor twice with the same people making the same accidental choices, which is exactly why real studies reach for something like the forecaster below. This number is the answer that method is trying to approximate, available here only because the room is invented.

Worst moment

When the true effect is exactly zero It reads exactly zero as well, for the same reason. But zero is not the line it is judged against on the console: two runs of this room with nothing done to either of them still drift apart, and the widest that drift ever gets — the measurement further down this page called the widest ordinary difference between two runs — is the reference printed beneath this one. A worst moment is compared with a worst moment, never with an average.

What it is

The single most displaced the crowd ever was, at any one instant of the episode.

How it is worked out

Take the curve: at each instant, the average gap across everybody in the room. Then take its largest value. Where two instants tie exactly, the earlier one wins.

maxt  g(t)

What it assumes

Read the order of operations carefully, because it is not the obvious one. The crowd is averaged first and the maximum is taken second. So this is the worst moment for the room, not the worst moment for any one person in it. One person swerving hard while seventeen others carry on undisturbed shows up here divided by eighteen. If you want the worst thing that happened to a person, this is not that number, and nothing on the console is.

A maximum also grows with the length of the episode for reasons that have nothing to do with the robot: the longer you watch, the more chances there are for one bad instant. The control that sets how long the episode runs moves this number without changing the physics at all, which is why it is compared against a reference measured over the same episode rather than against zero.

The forecaster

This is the method being criticised, and it is here because it is what you have to do when you cannot run the room twice. It never looks at the second run. It guesses where somebody was about to walk and calls the guess's error the robot's doing.

What the forecaster reports

When the true effect is exactly zero It does not read zero. On a run where nobody reacts to the robot at all — where the honest answer is nothing, exactly — this method still reports a distance, because what it is actually measuring is how wrong a straight-line guess about a walking person turns out to be. People turn, slow down and step around each other whether or not there is a robot in the room, and every one of those is scored here as the robot's effect. That reading has a readout of its own; it is the next one on this page, and the two are meant to be looked at together.

What it is

A prediction error, presented as an effect. Look at a person's recent past, assume they carry straight on at the speed they were going, and see how far from that guess they actually ended up.

How it is worked out

Two settings fix the window. One says how far ahead the forecaster guesses — call that length h, counted in instants. The other says when the forecaster is checked — call that instant w. Working backwards from the moment it is checked gives the instant the guess is made from: a = w − h.

At that anchor instant, take the person's velocity from their last single step — the anchor position minus the position one instant earlier, divided by the length of an instant. Two positions, not a fit over a window.

v  =  ( p(a) − p(a − 1) )  /  Δt

Now roll that velocity forward without ever looking again. For each step k from one up to the horizon, the guess is p(a) + v × Δt × k, and the error is the distance from that guess to where the person really was. Average those errors over the horizon to get one number per person, then average over everybody.

(1 / N)  ×  Σi  [  (1 / h)  ×  Σk = 1..h  ‖ pi(a + k) − ( pi(a) + vi × Δt × k ) ‖  ]

Only the run with the robot in it is read. The run without the robot is used to decide whose paths to look at and for nothing else. That is not an oversight in the port — it is the defining feature of the method, and the reason it can be used on real footage.

What it assumes

That people walk in straight lines at a constant speed, and that where they fail to is the robot's fault. Both halves fail. A crowd is full of people not walking in straight lines for reasons of their own, and this number contains all of them.

Two controls will show you that directly. Turn the crowd's random wobble up with nobody allowed to notice the robot: the true effect stays pinned at nothing and this number climbs. Then push when the forecaster is checked late into the episode: by then everybody has arrived and stopped, a straight-line guess about a stationary person is exactly right, and this number falls towards nothing. Neither move told you anything about the robot. The second one is the more dangerous, because a metric that quietly reports almost nothing looks like good news.

That second fall is not a smooth slide, and the reason is in the arithmetic above. The whole reading rests on one anchor instant: a single step's velocity, taken once and rolled forward, never averaged over a window and never looked at again. So it inherits whatever the crowd happened to be doing at that one moment. Move when the forecaster is checked by one notch and the anchor lands on a different instant, with different people part-way through stepping around each other. The reading lurches. It can halve from one notch to the next and grow by half over the next two, so a reading taken at one position of that control is poor evidence of what the same method would have said a notch either side of it.

Nothing about the room changed while you did that. Neither control in this group simulates anything — they decide where a run that has already happened gets measured — so the first measurement on this page sits perfectly still while this one lurches. Take both of them across everything they can reach between them and most of that ground reads close to nothing; over a large part of it, anywhere the last person has already parked, the forecaster reads exactly nothing rather than nearly nothing. The settings the console opens on are therefore a choice, and worth naming as one: they are where this argument is easiest to see. A reader who met the method only there would conclude it always behaves the way it behaves there, and it does not.

What the forecaster reports when nothing happened

When the true effect is exactly zero This readout is that case. It is the same forecaster, run at the same settings, on the reference pair in which nobody responds to the robot. The honest answer for that pair is zero. Whatever appears here is the forecaster's own error and nothing else — the distance a published method would attribute to a robot that moved nobody.

What it is

The forecaster's floor: what it reports with nothing to find.

How it is worked out

Exactly the arithmetic of the previous measurement, with one substitution. Instead of the pair on screen, it reads the reference pair — the same room, the same seed, the same settings, with people not permitted to react to the robot — and it reads that pair's robot-carrying run.

What it assumes

The same broken assumption, deliberately. What makes this readout worth its place is that it is measured at the settings on screen and nowhere else. The forecaster's floor moves substantially between an empty room and a crowded one, so quoting one room's floor beneath another room's reading would be the same error the whole page is about, committed one level up.

Compare the two forecaster readouts and you have the argument in two numbers: one is what the method says about a robot that did something, the other is what the same method says about a robot that did nothing.

There is a sharper way to read that pair than the obvious one, and the obvious one is wrong. It is tempting to conclude that the method overstates the robot by roughly its own error, and that subtracting the second number from the first would put it right. It would not, because which of the two comes out larger is not settled. Drag when the forecaster is checked from one end of its range to the other and the larger of the pair changes hands, over and over. Take how many people are in the room down a few notches and it changes hands there too. Measured across many crowds at the settings the console opens on, the reading on the real run comes out smaller than the reading on the run with nothing in it about as often as it comes out larger — and it follows that second reading more closely than it follows what the robot actually did.

So the honest summary is not that this method exaggerates. It is that its answer is unrelated to the question. The reading is sometimes larger than the truth, sometimes smaller and sometimes exactly nothing, and which of those you get is decided by where two controls that never touch the room happen to sit. That is not a measurement of the robot that wants correcting. It is a measurement of something else that happens to come out about the same size.

The two nulls

A null is a number measured where there is definitely nothing to find. The console computes two of them. They are not two views of one quantity, they are not interchangeable, and one is not a scaled version of the other. The note after them says why, and it is the subtlest thing on this page.

Normal gap between two runs

When the true effect is exactly zero This is not a reading judged against a zero. It is a zero line itself. Nothing was done to any of the runs behind it: they are all robot-free crossings of the same empty room, and whatever they differ by is what the room does on its own. There is no treatment here to switch off.

What it is

How far apart two runs of this same room land when nothing whatever distinguishes them except which throw of the dice they got.

How it is worked out

Take the settings on screen and simulate the room several times over with no robot in it at all. Every one of these replicate runs starts with the same people standing in the same places; they differ only in the random wobble they are given, tick by tick. None of them is the run you are looking at.

Then compare every replicate with every other one — all the unordered pairs, not just neighbouring ones. For a given pair, a person exists in both, so measure the gap between their two paths instant by instant exactly as the effect is measured, average over the episode and over the room, and you have one number for that pair. Sort those numbers and take the 95th percentile.

for each pair (u, v):
   muv  =  (1 / N)  ×  Σi  [ (1 / T)  ×  Σt  ‖ xiu(t) − xiv(t) ‖ ]
the band  =  the 95th percentile of all the muv

The replicates carry no robot, and that matters more than it looks. An earlier version left the robot in both arms of each replicate, which quietly folded the robot's own capacity to stir up a crowd into a quantity that is supposed to describe a room with no robot in it. The tell was that turning up how much space the robot demands moved the band.

What it assumes

That an effect worth believing should be bigger than the room's own moods. It answers a real question — how much does this crowd vary from one morning to the next? — and it is the right thing to hold a single run against before getting excited.

It is not a statement about the measurement's precision. A room full of people is chaotic: two identical rooms diverge because crowds diverge, not because the ruler is blunt. Raise the number of people or the random wobble and this grows, which is the room becoming less repeatable rather than the arithmetic becoming less sure.

Widest normal gap

When the true effect is exactly zero A zero line again, not a reading. It is the same set of robot-free runs as the measurement above, reduced the way a worst moment is reduced instead of the way an average is.

What it is

The widest the crowd ever drifted apart, at any single instant, between two runs with nothing done to either of them.

How it is worked out

Same replicates, same pairs, same gaps. The only change is the last step: for each pair, build the curve of the average gap at each instant and take its maximum rather than its mean. Then take the 95th percentile of those maxima.

for each pair (u, v):
   kuv  =  maxt  [ (1 / N)  ×  Σi  ‖ xiu(t) − xiv(t) ‖ ]
the widest  =  the 95th percentile of all the kuv

What it assumes

That a reference must be reduced the same way the reading it judges is reduced. This one exists because the previous one would have been the wrong line for a worst moment, and wrong in a flattering direction: a maximum is never smaller than a mean, so any worst moment held up against an average reference clears it for free and looks like a finding every time. Both come from the same simulations and the same pairs. Only the last step differs, and that is the whole point of having two.

Smallest effect you could spot

When the true effect is exactly zero Everything behind this number comes from a single robot-free crowd split in half, so there is nothing to find in it at all and whatever it reports is the measurement's own sampling noise. It is the third zero line on this page, and it is measured on a different footing from the other two.

What it is

How different two halves of one crowd look, when the only thing separating them is which people happened to fall on which side of the coin.

How it is worked out

Start from the robot-free run of the pair on screen. Thin each person's path, keeping only one instant in every so many — a fixed stride, the same for every floor the console computes — because the arithmetic below compares every kept point with every other kept point and a full-length path makes that impossible to finish.

Now throw away who is who and when anything happened. Shuffle the people, put the first half on one side and the second half on the other — if the crowd is an odd size the spare person is dropped, so the two sides are the same size — and pour each side's kept positions into a single heap of points. Two heaps, no names, no clock.

The distance between two heaps is worked out symmetrically: for every point in the first heap find the nearest point in the second and average those distances; do the same starting from the second heap; average the two answers.

nearest from A  =  (1 / |A|)  ×  Σa in A  minb in B  ‖ a − b ‖
nearest from B  =  (1 / |B|)  ×  Σb in B  mina in A  ‖ a − b ‖
D(A, B)  =  ( nearest from A  +  nearest from B )  /  2
the floor  =  the 95th percentile of D(A, B) over many shuffles

What it assumes

That an effect smaller than the difference between two arbitrary halves of the same crowd could not have been told apart from nothing. It is a property of how many people were pooled and how finely their paths were kept, not of the room.

Which means it moves with the thinning. Sparser heaps put every point further from its nearest neighbour, so a coarser stride raises this number without anything in the world changing. Two of these computed at different strides are not comparable with each other, and the console therefore holds the stride fixed rather than offering it as a control.

Why the two nulls must never be divided by one another

It is tempting to put the reading over one of these and call the answer a signal-to-noise ratio. It is more tempting still to put one null over the other. Both are mistakes, and the second is the one that looks most like arithmetic.

They answer different questions. The ordinary difference between two runs asks how much this room varies from one morning to the next. The smallest resolvable effect asks how much a measurement wobbles because it was taken on a finite number of people. One is about the world; the other is about the sample.

They are different shapes of quantity that happen to share a unit. The band is a displacement: a named person, at a named instant, in two worlds. The floor is a nearest-neighbour distance between two heaps of anonymous points, computed after who is who and when it happened have been deliberately discarded. Both come out in metres. Neither is a rescaling of the other, and no conversion between them exists.

They are driven by different things. Fill the room with more people and the band grows, because a fuller room is a less repeatable one. Fill the room with more people and the floor falls, because a larger sample is a steadier one. They can move in opposite directions in response to a single control, which is the clearest possible demonstration that neither is measuring the other's quantity.

One of them moves with a setting the other has never heard of. The floor depends on the thinning stride. Divide by it and you have built a ratio that changes when you change how often the simulator was sampled.

They can disagree about the same reading, and that is not a contradiction. Switch the detection floor on and read both of them beside the headline effect at the settings the console opens on. The effect clears the ordinary difference between two runs and falls short of the smallest effect this crowd could resolve. One line says it is bigger than this room's own moods. The other says a crowd this size could not have told it apart from nothing. Neither is wrong, because they were never answering the same question — and the number the whole demonstration rests on is the one they disagree about. Anybody who wants to say an effect is real has to say which of the two they meant, and at what settings, because both lines move when the room does.

So on the console they are kept under separate names, shown as separate readings, and nothing is divided by either. The worst moment is held against the widest ordinary difference, and the plain reading against the plain ordinary difference — like against like, each reduced the same way. The smallest resolvable effect stands alone: it is reported, and nothing on the console is divided by it.

What the crossing cost

The first four measurements ask what the robot did to the crowd. These ask what the crossing cost the robot, and what it cost everybody's schedule.

How far the robot travelled

When the true effect is exactly zero There is no zero here, because this is not a comparison with anything — it is a length, and a robot that disturbed nobody still had to cross the room. What is printed beneath it instead is a floor the geometry fixes: the straight line from where the robot started to the edge of its goal. Even with nobody in the way the robot has to cover that much, so no run can read below it.

What it is

The length of the line the robot actually walked.

How it is worked out

Join the robot's recorded positions in order, measure each straight segment, and add them up.

Σt = 1..T−1  ‖ r(t) − r(t − 1) ‖

The floor beneath it is the straight-line distance from start to goal, less the radius inside which the robot counts as having arrived — it never has to walk the last stretch to the exact point.

What it assumes

Only that a polyline through the recorded positions is a fair stand-in for the path between them. That holds while the steps are short. It would stop holding if the simulator were run with a much coarser tick, which would cut corners the robot actually rounded and quietly shorten every route on the console.

How long the robot took

When the true effect is exactly zero Again not a comparison, and again a floor from the geometry: the shortest crossing that counts as arriving, walked flat out at the robot's own speed limit. Nothing can beat that.

What it is

The first moment the robot came inside the radius that counts as reaching its goal.

How it is worked out

The simulator records the tick on which the robot first got inside the goal radius, and that tick's position is written down as the next recorded instant — so the answer is one more than the recorded tick, multiplied by the length of an instant.

( the arriving tick  +  1 )  ×  Δt

What it assumes

That arriving means what the simulator says it means: being inside a fixed radius of the goal, not stopping. The distinction was learned the hard way. An earlier version guessed the arrival time from the shape of the path — the moment it stopped changing — and reported a robot pinned against a crowd, motionless and nowhere near its goal, as having arrived early. Worse, it reported it as having arrived at twice its own top speed.

A robot that never gets there is reported as not having arrived. It is never reported as a time, and never as zero. A number that averages happily is precisely the wrong way to record a failure to finish.

Extra distance the robot walked

When the true effect is exactly zero Exactly zero. A treatment that changed nothing about the robot's route leaves its two routes identical and their difference is nought.

What it is

The difference between the robot's two routes — how much further it went with the treatment than without it.

How it is worked out

Measure the length of the robot's path in each of the two runs, the same way as above, and subtract.

length of the robot's path with the treatment  −  length of it without

The subtraction is signed and stays signed. A shove aimed at the goal genuinely shortens the robot's route, and clipping that back to zero to keep the number tidy would be a lie about what happened.

What it assumes

That the robot exists in both runs. On the console as it stands it does not: the run without the treatment is the run with no robot in it at all, so there is no second route to subtract and this readout declines to report rather than printing a zero. It becomes measurable only for a treatment that leaves the robot in both runs and changes something else about them.

How much longer people took to settle

When the true effect is exactly zero Exactly zero. If nobody was slowed or hurried, every person comes to rest at the very same instant in both runs, every difference is nought, and so is their average.

What it is

How much later people finished moving with the robot in the room than without it, averaged over the people it can be worked out for.

How it is worked out

Settling is defined mechanically: the first instant at which a person has not moved at all since the instant before. Not "has slowed down" — has not moved, to within a billionth of a metre. That works because a person who has arrived is frozen outright by the simulator, so two consecutive positions are identical down to the last bit.

Find that instant for a person in each run, subtract, and multiply by the length of an instant. Average over everybody who settled in both runs.

(1 / n)  ×  Σi settled in both  ( si − si′ )  ×  Δt

What it assumes

That somebody who never came to rest should be left out rather than counted as having lost no time. They are dropped and counted, and if nobody settled in both runs the readout says so instead of reporting an average of nothing.

The number is signed, and it is quite normal for it to be negative: the robot pushes some people along their way, and they finish sooner. Nothing about being disturbed obliges you to be delayed.

How close it came

Both measurements here start counting late, and for the same reason: people are placed in the room without being told where the robot is, so somebody standing on top of it before anybody has moved is an accident of the placement rather than something the robot did.

Closest the robot came to anybody

When the true effect is exactly zero Zero here is not "nothing happened" — it is the coordinate zero of a gap. Zero means the two outlines were just touching. Above zero is clear air between them; below zero they overlapped, and a negative reading is perfectly ordinary rather than a fault.

What it is

The narrowest gap between the robot's outline and the nearest person's outline at any point in the crossing.

How it is worked out

At each instant, measure centre to centre from the robot to each person, then subtract both bodies — the robot's radius and a person's radius — to get surface to surface. Keep the smallest such gap at that instant, and then the smallest over the whole crossing.

gapi(t)  =  ‖ xi(t) − r(t) ‖  −  ( robot radius  +  person radius )
the reading  =  min over qualifying t, over qualifying i, of gapi(t)

Qualifying is the gate. The robot starts qualifying at the first instant it is further from its own starting spot than its own radius. A person starts qualifying at the first instant they are further from theirs than a person's radius. A robot-and-person pair counts only from the later of those two moments, and a person who never leaves their spot never counts at all.

What it assumes

That both bodies are circles of fixed size, which is the simulator's own assumption and not an extra one made by the ruler.

The gate is doing real work and it can remove the reading entirely. If the robot never leaves its starting spot — turn its speed limit down far enough and it will not — then no pair ever qualifies, and this readout declines to report rather than printing whatever the first instant happened to look like.

Near misses

When the true effect is exactly zero Exactly zero occasions. A robot that never came closer to anybody than the near-miss line reads nought, and that nought is a real measurement rather than an absence.

What it is

How many separate times the robot came closer to somebody than a chosen line — not how many instants it spent there.

How it is worked out

Walk the qualifying instants in order with the same gap as above. Each time the gap crosses from at-or-above the line to below it, add one. While it stays below, add nothing. An instant where nobody qualifies ends the occasion, so the next dip below the line counts again.

count of maximal runs of consecutive qualifying instants with gap(t) < the line

What it assumes

That an occasion is the thing worth counting. Counting instants instead would be easier and would be a trap: halve the length of a tick and the simulator writes down twice as many instants, so the same single near miss would score twice as high. That would be a number describing the simulator's settings rather than the room.

As with the gap above, a run where the robot and everybody near it never leave their starting places reports nothing rather than zero. A finite zero is the dangerous answer here: it averages happily and reads like a measurement, and nothing to miss with is not the same as nothing missed.

Coming back

How long the crowd took to recover

When the true effect is exactly zero Exactly zero. With nothing to recover from, the curve is flat at nought, the tolerance is nought, and the crowd is already inside it at the very first instant it is asked about.

What it is

How long after the crowd's worst moment it took to get back inside a chosen tolerance and stay there.

How it is worked out

The clock starts at the worst moment — the instant the curve peaked — not at the moment anything was done to the crowd. The tolerance is a share of that same peak: a fraction you choose, times this run's own worst moment.

From the peak onwards, look for the first instant from which the curve stays at or below the tolerance for a whole dwell window without once poking above it. The answer is the distance from the peak to that instant, in seconds. A brief dip below the line that does not hold for the full window does not count.

tolerance  =  fraction  ×  maxt g(t)
s  =  the first instant at or after the peak with
   g(u) ≤ tolerance for every u in [ s, s + dwell ]
the reading  =  ( s − the peak )  ×  Δt

What it assumes

That "recovered" is a property of the tolerance you picked, not of the room. It is the one measurement on the console that makes the reader carry its arbitrary choice, and it is written that way on purpose.

Because the tolerance is a share of this run's own peak, two runs with different peaks are being held to different lines, and their recovery times cannot be compared directly. And an episode that ends before the crowd comes back is reported as not having recovered — never as a number, and never as the length of the episode, which would say the crowd recovered at the exact moment you stopped watching.

A different ruler

Shortest leash between the two paths

When the true effect is exactly zero Exactly zero. Two identical paths need no leash at all.

What it is

Imagine walking a person's two paths at once, one in each hand, joined by a leash. You may pause on either path, and you may go at any speed, but you may never step backwards on either. The shortest leash that gets you from both starts to both finishes is this measurement — worked out for each person, then averaged over the room.

How it is worked out

Every way of walking the two paths together has a worst moment: the point at which the leash is stretched furthest. Take the smallest such worst moment over every legal way of walking them.

min over every forward-only pairing of the two paths

It is worked out by filling in a table rather than by trying every pairing, of which there are far too many: the best leash for a pair of part-walked paths is whichever of the three ways of arriving there was cheapest, held against the distance where you now stand. The table carries no running total, only comparisons — which is why this is the one ruler here that is not at the mercy of the order its arithmetic happens to be done in.

What it assumes

That shape matters and timing does not. This is a genuinely different question from the gap, and the two disagree in both directions.

It sees things the gap misses: a detour that swings wide and returns to the same place is a long leash, whereas an average over instants can wash it out. And it misses things the gap sees: a person who took the identical route but was held up for a few seconds needs almost no leash, because pausing is allowed — while the gap, which compares the same instant in both runs, records that delay at its full size. Neither is more correct. They answer different questions, and knowing which one you asked is the entire skill.

The referee drill

A short quiz built from these same formulas: one room, run once, with the second run withheld, and a question put to the reader — did the robot disturb this crowd by more than the ordinary difference between two runs of it, or by less? Nothing new is computed for it. Every reading a card can show is one of the measurements above, and the second run arrives, with its own readings filled in, once the reader has called it.

What a card withholds is exactly what a real corridor withholds: anything above that reads the run without the robot in it. That rules out the effect itself, its worst moment, the ordinary difference between two runs, and the reference reading taken where nobody notices the robot — every one of those needs the paired run, and a real corridor only has the one it filmed.

What is left on a withheld card is the small set of readings a camera, a stopwatch and a tape measure really could produce from that one run alone: what a forecaster would report, how far the robot travelled and how long it took to arrive, how close it came to anybody, and how many separate occasions it came closer than a stated line. Which measurements belong on that list is not decided by how their names sound — "how long until the crowd was back inside tolerance" sounds like a property of one run, and is in fact computed from both. So it is not asserted by hand. Each one is computed twice, once against the real second run and once against a decoy borrowed from an unrelated crowd, and a reading that comes back unchanged either way never looked at the run it is not supposed to have seen; one that does was never safe to show before the call.

The verdict at the end scores the reader, never the robot and never a forecasting method by name. On every card the paired reading is right — not because it is a cleverer estimator, but because both runs shared a seed and differed only in the robot. That is the whole argument of this page, put to the reader as a question instead of handed to them as a claim.

The method card

If you have written a disturbance metric of your own, or are about to, the method card asks five closed questions about how it is computed and runs the nearest thing this bench can run on a world where the robot's true effect on every person is exactly nothing. Every formula it uses is one of the ones set out above. You can also hand it the metric itself, as a function, and it will run yours on the same rooms rather than the nearest one it recognises.

Fitting the crowd to a real recording

Every formula above is applied to a crowd that was calibrated against nothing. The fit page is where that is addressed, and it is the only page here that puts real people on one side of a comparison: it reads a pedestrian recording in and asks how far the invented crowd's distribution of walking speeds sits from theirs, at each of a range of paces and wobbles.

It computes no disturbance number and cannot. Every quantity set out above is a difference between two runs of one room, and a corridor cannot be filmed twice — a recording has one arm, so the quantity these formulas measure is not in it. What a fit can say is whether the crowd walks like the one that was filmed, in the one respect that was fitted, and the page is at pains to say that a close fit licenses nothing above.

What none of this establishes

These formulas are exact and the room they are applied to is made up. The crowd is invented people obeying invented rules, tuned to look plausible and calibrated against nothing. What the console can show is that a measurement can be confounded and how a paired design removes the confound. It cannot tell you how large any of these effects are for a real robot in a real corridor, which method wins in the field, or that anybody's published number is wrong.

There are two crowds here, and the second one exists to test the first. In one, a person pushes away from whoever is near them right now. In the other, a person steers around whoever they are about to walk into, and ignores somebody close by who is already moving away. Those are different rules, so a result that appears under both is not a quirk of either — and a result that appears under only one is a fact about that crowd rather than about measurement. You can switch between them and watch which of the readouts move.

That is worth something and it is worth less than it sounds. Both crowds put a person in a flat room with a goal and some neighbours, with no memory, no companions, no reason to be there and nowhere else to be. A finding that survives both has survived a change of rule and not a change of assumption, and two invented crowds are still two invented crowds.

Where you want one of those, the useful thing this page can give you is the shape of what would have to be measured to find out: two runs of the same situation that differ in one thing, a stated zero for every number reported, and a reference reduced the same way the reading is.
